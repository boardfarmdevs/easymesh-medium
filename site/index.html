easymesh-medium

How the labs' radio medium works

Both EasyMesh optimizer labs run real Wi-Fi software, the RDK stack in one and prplMesh in the other, on radios that have no antennas. Something has to decide, for every frame, which radios hear it and how well. This is the story of that something: a kernel's worth of virtual radios, one referee process in the middle, and a floor plan that tells the referee what the air in a house is like.

Why a lab needs a medium

An EasyMesh optimizer decides which access point each client should be on, and when to move it. To develop one you need a house: a gateway, a few extenders, dozens of phones and laptops, and people walking from the kitchen to the bedroom while the optimizer watches. You need to run the same walk a hundred times and get the same air each time, and you need to run it on two different EasyMesh implementations to see whether a policy is good or merely lucky.

Real radios give you none of that. The air in a lab is shared with the neighbours, a phone in a pocket is never in the same place twice, and a hundred clients cost a hundred devices. So the labs run everything in containers: the controller, the extenders and every client run their ordinary Wi-Fi software, unmodified, on virtual radios. The radios are real enough for that software. What they lack is a world around them. The medium is that world.

The rest of this page follows a frame from the moment a container sends it, and then builds up the room around it.

Radios without antennas

Linux has a driver for exactly this: mac80211_hwsim. Each instance is a complete 802.11 radio as far as the rest of the kernel is concerned. hostapd can run an access point on it, wpa_supplicant can scan and associate with it, OneWifi and prplMesh can configure it. Move one into a container's network namespace and that container has a Wi-Fi card.

On its own, hwsim connects every radio to every other one perfectly. A frame sent by one radio arrives at all the others on the same channel, every time, at the same signal. That is a fine way to test that two programs can talk, and a useless way to test a steering decision: if every access point is equally good everywhere, there is nothing to decide.

The labs use a patched hwsim build: it carries 6 GHz channels, several channels on one radio at once (multichannel), the channel surveys that let a stack report channel utilization, and an optional in-kernel medium for experiments. The important hook is upstream, though: hwsim can hand every frame to a userspace process instead of delivering it itself. That process is the referee.

One referee for every frame

When a container transmits, mac80211 passes the frame to hwsim, and hwsim sends it over generic netlink to wmediumd, with the transmitting radio's identity, the frequency and the rates the sender's rate control would try. wmediumd is one process for every radio in the lab. It does not know what a controller or a client is. It knows radios, frequencies, and how well each radio hears each other one, and it answers one question per frame and receiver: does this arrive, and at what signal?

The answer is not a label on a packet. A frame that wmediumd drops is never seen by the receiver; a frame it delivers arrives with a signal level the receiver's driver reports like any other. So everything above the radio behaves as it would in a house: scans find weaker access points or none, retries and rate fallback happen, a client at the edge loses its association, and the EasyMesh controller learns all of it through its normal reports.

Why not just fake the RSSI?

Patching the number a client or a controller reports would change one field and nothing else. The medium changes what happens: management frames, data, multicast and acknowledgements all succeed or fail by the same model, so rate control, scanning, roaming and the controller's metrics follow from the air instead of contradicting it.

The link matrix

At the centre of wmediumd is a table: for every ordered pair of radios, the signal-to-noise ratio of the link from one to the other. It is directed, because links are not always symmetric: a laptop with a weaker transmitter is heard less well by the access point than it hears the access point. And it can be frequency-qualified: one radio serving 2.4, 5 and 6 GHz at once gets a different value on each band, because the bands do not travel equally far.

The matrix starts from a file that lists every radio the lab created and a baseline value for every pair. After that it lives in memory and changes while the lab runs, as the next sections show. The SNR is the whole description of a link: wmediumd's noise floor is fixed at −91 dBm, so a link at 40 dB is received at about −51 dBm and one at 10 dB at about −81 dBm.

From signal to loss

A link's SNR does not decide a frame by itself. A frame sent at a high data rate needs a much cleaner signal than the same frame at a robust low rate, and a long frame is more likely to be hit by an error than a short one. wmediumd turns SNR, rate and frame length into a packet error probability using curves for the 802.11 modulations, then draws a random number to decide this particular frame.

This is why a client that walks away from an access point slows down before it disconnects, and why the controller's link metrics (data rates, retries, the signal it reports) move together in the lab the way they do in a house.

Airtime and channels

Deciding whether a frame arrives is half the job; the other half is when. A radio cannot transmit while the channel is busy, and every frame takes time on the air. wmediumd keeps a queue per radio and per 802.11 access category (voice, video, best effort, background) and schedules each frame with the waits the standard prescribes: an idle gap before transmitting, a random backoff, the frame's airtime at its rate, a short gap, the acknowledgement.

The labs' patches make this per frequency. Upstream wmediumd assumed one channel for everyone; a mesh does not work that way. The gateway serves three bands at once, the extenders' backhaul runs on its own 5 GHz channel, and a client is on one channel at a time. So the patched daemon learns the frequency of every transmission, delivers only to radios on the same centre frequency, and keeps each frequency's queues separate: a 2.4 GHz beacon cannot hold up 5 GHz traffic.

Airtime is also what the stacks measure. Channel utilization, the BSS load an access point advertises and the survey a controller asks for come from the time each frequency was busy, which the medium accounts for and the patched hwsim reports through the normal channel-survey interface.

Changing the air while it runs

A matrix fixed at startup describes a house where nobody moves. To make a client walk, the matrix has to change while the lab is running, without restarting anything: a restart would drop every association and teach the experiment nothing.

The labs' wmediumd has a control socket for this. A change is sent as a generation: a set of link values that apply together, at one instant, or not at all. Half a step of a walk never exists. After each generation the sender reads the values back and compares them; a scenario that cannot confirm what it applied stops instead of producing results about air it did not create. When the scenario ends, it restores the exact values it found, and checks those too.

Rooms: a floor plan becomes numbers

Nobody wants to write a thousand link values by hand. The medium's configurator, wmdcfg, computes them from two small files. The layout is the house: its size, where the gateway and extenders stand, where clients sit, the walls and how much each one costs. The mobility file is what happens: who walks where and when, who appears and disappears. Together they are a world, and the configurator compiles a world into a plan with every directed link's SNR on every band at every tick.

The model is deliberately simple and reproducible: a reference signal per band at one metre, log-distance path loss, and a fixed loss for every wall the straight line between two radios crosses.

SNR(band) = reference(band)
          − 10 · path_loss_exponent · log10(distance / 1 m)
          − Σ wall losses crossed
          + transmitter adjustment(band)
          + seeded shadowing (zero in the standard home)
                                                 clamped to [−20, 60] dB

The standard home is 20 by 14 metres with a gateway in the middle, four extenders in the corners, ten clients that stay put and four interior walls of 5 dB each. Its reference signals are 50 dB at 5 GHz, 54 at 2.4 and 47 at 6, with a path loss exponent of 2.2. Here is its shortest room, one-client handover: a laptop starts next to extender 1 and walks eight metres towards the gateway in sixteen seconds.

A room plan is computed once and checked in, with a hash of its inputs and of its content: a change to the model or to a room that does not regenerate the plans fails the medium's CI. Three families of rooms exist: the standard rooms (27), the same rooms with a wired extender (31), and the rooms with two OpenSync pods as access points (31). They cover walks and crossings, crowds arriving at once, clients hovering at a border, extenders failing and coming back, band changes, asymmetric links and traffic. Backhaul links follow the geometry too in the rooms that ask for it, so an extender can lose its parent and pick another one.

The air follows the room, not the roam

There is a tempting shortcut in writing a steering test: "make the current access point weak and the other one strong". It is wrong, and the medium does not allow it. The moment the client roams, "current" means the other access point, and the rule rewrites the air around the decision it was supposed to test.

So the medium keys every link to physical radios, through roles fixed for the whole run: extender 1 is a particular radio in a particular container, wherever its clients go. In the chart above, the two lines are the same whether the client stays with extender 1 for the whole walk, roams at second 11, or roams back and forth. Association is something the experiment observes and records. It is never an input to the air.

Keeping cause and effect apart

The same room can test doing nothing, a manual steer, the controller's own logic and an external optimizer, because the stimulus never depends on the decision. That is what makes two runs, or two stacks, comparable.

Who is who

wmediumd does not query the containers. It loads the list of radios once, from a file the lab generates by asking each running container for its radio's permanent address. hwsim names the transmitting radio with that address plus one bit (0x40 on the first byte), so 02:00:00:00:0e:00 in a container is 42:00:00:00:0e:00 to the medium. Free radios in the pool are left out: they would only receive copies of every broadcast.

One radio carries many addresses at run time: an extender's private and IoT access points, its backhaul access point, its backhaul station, each on its own frequency. wmediumd learns these as frames flow, from the transmitter address inside each frame and the frequency hwsim reports. A new BSSID or a client that associates needs no restart. Only a radio that joins or leaves the lab does.

Containers that are not one of the lab's own roles can join as guests. The EMOSA option of the RDK lab uses this for OpenSync pods: a container marked as a medium guest gets all its radios into the matrix, and can pin a few links of its own, such as a pod's fixed backhaul to its gateway tunnel.

Watching the medium

A medium that decides everything invisibly is hard to trust. Three windows look into it, and none of them can change it.

  • wmediumd Console, a read-only web application that every lab serves. It shows the radios in 3D, the directed matrix as wmediumd actually holds it, which pairs are carrying frames, per-frame-type counters, airtime and load per channel, and the identity of the configuration in force. It reads a host-only telemetry socket; it has no way to write.
  • The room viewer, the house in 3D: the walls, the access points, the clients moving, their links coloured by quality and the roams as they happen. The labs' room service serves it live; the labs' sites carry a copy that previews the rooms without a lab.
  • A metrics socket that the stacks' radio layers use to report what a real radio would measure: the received signal of a client, channel surveys and BSS load. The controller then gets its link metrics through its own protocol, as it would in a house.

The repository also holds the controller's topology page that both labs serve: one page for both stacks, with a profile for each. It lives here because it follows the room and shares the viewer's room projection and signal meters.

One medium, two labs

The RDK lab and the prplMesh lab build their medium from this repository at a pinned commit. Same daemon, same patches, same rooms, same compiled plans: when a policy behaves differently on the two stacks, the difference is in the stacks.

A tool picks its stack from an explicit option, an environment variable, or the controller container it finds running; it never guesses between two labs. A change to the medium is requalified in both.

What it does not model

The medium is built for comparing steering decisions under the same, reproducible air. It is not a calibrated radio simulator, and it says so:

  • Channels are isolated by exact centre frequency. Partial overlap and adjacent-channel interference are not modelled.
  • The loss curves are the legacy OFDM and CCK ones. HT and VHT rates map to the nearest of them; there is no error model for HE, EHT, channel width, guard interval, spatial streams, beamforming or multi-link operation.
  • Same-frequency interference accounting exists but is off in the labs' baseline; there is no random fading in the rooms, by design.
  • Signal levels are model values, SNR minus a fixed −91 dBm noise floor, not calibrated hardware RSSI.
  • An extender that the air cuts off is unreachable, not deleted: when a controller removes it from its topology is the controller's own ageing behaviour, which is one of the things the labs measure.

The medium's documents go further: what each RF property means, which rooms show it, and what is qualified live and what is not.

Everything builds and is tested without a lab: the CI builds and self-tests the daemon, regenerates every room plan and compares it, and runs the configurator's, the viewer's, the console's and the topology page's tests. No built binary is ever committed; each lab builds the daemon and the console from the commit it pins.

easymesh-medium is one of the two core components of the EasyMesh labs; the optimizer is what the rooms are built to test. The source and the full documentation are in the repository.
